How age grading works

1 The big idea

Picture the fastest runner in the world at your exact age and gender. The tables hold the time that runner could run at each distance: the standard. Your age grade is how close you came to it.

age grade = standard for your ageyour time × 100

100% means you matched the best time ever run at your age. 50% means you took twice as long. Because each age has its own standard, a 30-year-old and a 60-year-old can be compared fairly.

Example

A 50-year-old man runs an 8K in 30:00 (1800 s). His standard is 23:38 (1418 s).

1418 ÷ 1800 × 100 = 78.8%
Same grade, different runners

2 Where each age's standard comes from

For every age, the best time ever run is collected: the single-age bests, gathered by Tom Bernhard and, before him, the ARRS (Association of Road Racing Statisticians). Alan Jones then turns them into the tables. Those records are bumpy, so a smooth curve is drawn that is never slower than any of them. Dividing the fastest standard by your age's standard gives your age factor: 1.0 in the prime years, lower on either side. A 60-year-old man's 5K factor is 0.81, so a 5K about 19% slower than the open world record is world class at 60.

The curve is built from simple pieces joined smoothly: a steep climb through childhood, a flat top around the world record, a slow steady decline through the masters years, then a decline that speeds up in the 70s and beyond.

3 Distances in between: interpolation

Only four distances are raced often enough at every age to fit a curve on their own: 5K, 10K, the half marathon and the marathon. Every distance in between is interpolated: its age factor is a blend of the two key distances on either side. How much of each depends on where it sits between them on a log scale (distances are compared by ratio, so 5K to 10K is the same step as 10K to 20K). That position is called u, from 0 at the shorter distance to 1 at the longer one.

4 At the edges: extrapolation

Sometimes there is nothing on the far side to blend with, so the tables carry on past the data:

  • Very old and very young ages. Records thin out past about 90, and some ages have none at all. The curve simply keeps its shape past the last record (the grey band in the first graph), so those standards are a best estimate. Children's ages are handled slightly differently too, and are the least certain part of the tables.
  • Beyond the marathon. For 50K and longer, the age factor is kept at the marathon's value: a 60-year-old man's factor is 0.814 at the marathon and 0.814 at 50K. Only the open standard changes with the distance.

5 What this site does

  • Your age on race day and your gender pick the row; the race distance picks the column.
  • Records keep improving, so the tables are revised every few years. The version used follows the race date, so older results keep the grade they had (table below).
  • A result is graded only when its distance is one of the table's distances. The site does no interpolation of its own, so an unusual distance has no age grade.
  • Results with more than one runner, multisport races and results that are not record eligible have no age grade.
